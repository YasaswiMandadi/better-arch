Season 02 · Conversation 33 — Architecture X Media Studies

Medium of Discovery

Kamna Malik · Brand & Communications Strategist · Design Media Veteran · London/India

Seventeen years across Indian design and architecture media — editorial, fairs, brand-side communications. Held senior roles in the era when ELLE Decor, A+D and the early India Design ID set the editorial weather. Moved on from the corporate structure roughly three years before the conversation; based in London at the time of recording. Speaks here as somebody who watched the discovery economy of design media end in real time, and who is willing to say so out loud.

Synopsis

A reading of the conversation

Kamna Malik joins Shubhayan M. from London with seventeen years inside Indian design and architecture media behind her. The conversation opens with a long question and stays with one short one. The fairs, the magazines, the awards circuit, the panel slots — were these ever really about discovery, and if they were, are they still? She remembers when <em>India Design ID</em> and ACETECH were two of two, when writers flew between cities to interview principal architects, when an editorial filter actually said no.

The diagnosis starts with the fair floor itself. Attendance used to mean discovery — pen drives, catalogues, brand cards as ammunition, no other way to find a new material or a new name. <em>"It’s definitely not for discovery,"</em> she says now; the same booths and panels have been emptied of that function and refilled with another, being seen, being projected, maintaining relationships with the media houses that cover the fair. The international counter-examples — Salone del Mobile, London Design Festival, Design Miami — are offered not as uncommercialised ideals but as <em>cultural moments</em> that still move an industry, against Indian shows that, in her account, sustain only the booths.

The conversation gets sharper from there. Discovery has been replaced by optics; <em>me-toos</em> have multiplied as one successful format gets copied by ten studios across geographies; the <em>dal-tarka</em> metaphor lands cleanly — same dish, every day, until the audience is done. She names the bubble and refuses to soften the call: magazines <em>"have become glorified catalogues,"</em> because today you can buy a feature the way you once could only earn one. She also refuses to play expert about everything. Pressed on how a tier-2-city studio breaks in today, she says she genuinely does not know, and explains exactly why the old answer — write to the editor, get judged on merit — has stopped working: talent is no longer the only currency. The honesty of <em>I don’t know</em> is the most useful thing said in that stretch.

The take comes in the close, and it is programmatic. Architects and studios should stop seeking validation from magazines; they should hire content people in-house and control their own narrative, because an outside agency, however talented, does not sit in the office and does not know its daily nuances. Magazines, in turn, have one option left — reinvent, structurally rather than cosmetically, or become glorified catalogues, which the West is already rehearsing in its own shutdowns and pulled titles. Self-publishing for studios; structural rethink for media. The episode is the rare AXM conversation where the prescription is clear and the speaker is willing to wear the cost of saying it, flagging before she says the hardest lines that this will get her into trouble, and saying them anyway.

Themes

What the conversation argues

Discovery as the lost function

Kamna Malik's central claim is structural and small. The fairs and magazines that shaped Indian design media for two decades existed because they did one thing nobody else could do: they let architects, builders, students walk in and find products, materials, brands, names. The pen drives, the catalogues, the trips to ACETECH and India Design ID in 2010, 2011, 2012 — these were the apparatus of an information market. Discovery was not an aspiration of the format; it was the format.

The pivot comes when SHM presses for one word: from discovery, what has it become? "Today's become about optics," KM says, and the conversation reorganises around the contrast (t-37). The same physical event — booths, panels, footfall — now does a different job: being seen, being projected, maintaining relationships with media houses and brands. "It's definitely not for discovery. It's not about, oh, you know what? I should go there because I'm gonna find something new — that is not the agenda anymore" (t-30). The brand sales-force does discovery work for eleven and a half months of the year by phone and email; the four days of the fair are for something else.

The diagnosis bleeds into every other theme. Without the discovery function, the me-too problem (Theme 3) follows automatically — same booths, same brands, same template across geographies. Without the discovery function, the editorial filter (Theme 4) has nothing to filter for. And without the discovery function, the access question for tier-2 studios (Theme 2) cannot be answered by the old recipe of doing good work and waiting to be found. Discovery is the function whose loss explains the others.

Talent is no longer the only currency

Pressed on how a five-year-old studio in a tier-2 city — Indore, Chandigarh, Hyderabad in her old examples — gets onto a panel or a magazine spread today, KM does the harder thing and refuses the recipe. "I genuinely don't know," she says, twice (t-131, t-133). The honesty is not a dodge; it is the answer. The recipe she could have given — call up the editor, send the project, get judged on the editorial aesthetic — described a system that is no longer in operation.

The structural diagnosis arrives in one line: "Talent is not the only currency anymore" (t-137). The sentence is sharper than it sounds because it inverts the moral economy under which most architectural-merit conversations are conducted. If access were a function of talent, sustained good work would eventually break in. KM's claim is that access is now a function of multiple currencies — relationships, PR spend, geography, willingness to play the optics game — and talent is one currency among them, not the dominant one.

The implication for young practices is structural rather than tactical. KM offers no five-step playbook because the system is not playbook-shaped; she offers instead the same instruction she will repeat at the close — stop seeking validation — and warns that the vanity of the cover, when it doesn't come, will demoralise people who deserve better. "Don't let that become the be-all or end-all because then you may not, because when you don't get into those spaces, it can be very demotivating" (t-142).

The herd, the me-too, the dal-tarka

The cultural mechanism that produces homogenisation gets a name and a metaphor. The name is herd mentality — "one entity has gotten it right, there are 10 others who've gotten up and said, we will do exactly the same thing" (t-80). The metaphor is dal-tarka: "if you're served dal-tarka every single day, how many days are you going to have the same dal-tarka? There will come a time when you'll be like, you know what? I'm done with this" (t-86). The Hindi switch carries hospitality, household routine and exhaustion in one image, and does the analytical work that several paragraphs of structural critique would have done less efficiently.

The mechanism operates at multiple scales. At the level of fairs, one successful template begets ten replications across geographies (t-85). At the level of design itself, "if one architect or one designer is doing beige and that person becomes popular or that person gets featured, everybody wants to start adopting to it" (t-148). KM is careful to refuse the single-cause story here — she names customers and consumers as co-authors, with their Pinterest libraries and Instagram references, and locates the answer in "courage to stay true to who you are" rather than in any structural fix.

The contrast set is international and explicit: Salone del Mobile, London Design Festival, Design Miami. "These are not just fairs, these are cultural moments that create a certain impact. They create a certain narrative in the industry. They bring about a change" (t-84). The argument is not that these events are uncommercialised — KM concedes they are — but that they sustain interventions, talents and emotions alongside the booths. The Indian shows, by her account, sustain only the booths.

Magazines as catalogues, not curators

The institution under autopsy is the design magazine, and the autopsy is conducted on its own historical terms. KM defends the old model first — the ten pages of advertising that used to frustrate the young SHM existed because "if those clients didn't pay money, the copies would not get printed and there was no other way to generate money on a magazine" (t-57). Subscriptions never funded the work; Filmfare and Femina were partial exceptions in their own segment.

What has been lost is not the ad model — that was always there — but the editorial filter and the seasoned editor it required. "Today they're not about content. They have become glorified catalogues because today you can buy an article, you can buy a feature, you can buy anything to get anything published because everything is about the economic study" (t-122). The phrase glorified catalogues is the hardest sentence in the episode and the one that would, by KM's own warning, get her into trouble. Editorial filter as the lost mechanism (t-123) explains the catalogue diagnosis without making it personal: when nothing says no, everything is yes.

The bleed into the closing prescription is direct. Magazines, KM says in the close, will either reinvent themselves or "only become more and more glorified catalogues" (t-178). The Western precedent — "the best of the magazines are shutting down, the best of the media companies are pulling the plug on some of their best titles" (t-180) — is named as a forecast. "It's a matter of time that's gonna start happening in India" (t-181). The diagnosis and the prognosis collapse into one sentence.

Stop seeking validation; become your own brand

The closing prescription is two-part and unusually clear for a conversation that has spent forty minutes refusing to give recipes. "Architects and design studios need to stop seeking validation from magazines. They themselves need to start becoming brands in themselves today" (t-174). The instruction is not 'do better PR'; it is 'control your own narrative', operationalised as a hire. "Studios need to start hiring content people within their teams. Like today, a design studio has architects, designers, operational people, project managers. Studios need to start hiring content people" (t-175).

The internal-vs-external argument is given on operational rather than ideological grounds. "Getting an external agency to do your social media or to create content for you will not work in the long run because no matter how talented that agency is, they are not sitting in your office. They don't know the daily workings and the nuances" (t-175). The international parallel — large practices abroad with in-house communications functions — is the implicit model. The phrase free advice is doing work too: it marks this as not the consultant's pitch she could have made, but the thing she would say at a dinner.

The second half of the prescription is for the magazines themselves. "If they do not reinvent themselves, they are going to only become glorified catalogues" (t-178). KM's version of reinvention is structural rather than aesthetic: positioning teams differently, looking at talent differently, looking at operations differently. The two prescriptions are not separate — they describe the same shift from one side and the other. If studios become their own publishers, the magazines lose their monopoly on validation; if magazines do not reinvent, they lose the function altogether. The episode closes on this two-sided ultimatum, delivered without rhetorical heat.

Lived experience

What it feels like to be the practitioner

To sit inside this conversation is to watch a trade autopsy its own instruments while naming the moment each one stopped working. What the listener feels first is the relief of hearing the fair, the sponsored award and the glorified catalogue described for what they have become, rather than what they are still advertised as. Kamna Malik does not perform radicalism; she states a loss plainly, the way someone states a loss they watched happen slowly rather than one that surprised them. The texture of the hour is her refusal to pretend the old recipe still works — she says twice, unhedged, that she does not know how a tier-2-city studio breaks in today, and that admission carries more weight than a confident wrong answer would have. The <em>dal-tarka</em> line does in one breath what several paragraphs of structural critique would have done more slowly, and it is delivered with the same plainness as everything else she says, which is part of why it lands. She flags the professional cost of her own candour before incurring it — <em>"this is gonna be an episode that will get me into a lot of trouble"</em> — and then continues anyway, through the harder claims about magazines, about the herd, about what studios and platforms have stopped doing for the people who make the work. The close is not despair; it is instruction, offered as something closer to free advice than to a consulting pitch, and it asks both sides of the trade — the studios and the magazines — to do a different, harder thing than either has been doing.

Sentiment register

How the conversation feels

The episode reads as measured, specific and plainly stated, closer to diagnosis than to performance. The dominant note is a composed defiance: the glorified catalogue, the herd mechanism and the exhausted fair format are named without raised voice, and the admission that speaking will cost her something precedes rather than follows the hardest lines. Candour runs high — twice she says plainly that she does not know an answer rather than inventing one — and the register stays generous toward the people the diagnosis might otherwise read as scolding, naming customers and consumers as co-authors of the herd rather than villains of it. The locatedness is strong, grounded in named fairs, named cities and a specific seventeen-year vantage, and the conversation closes on instruction rather than complaint.

Criticality compass

Independent and critical

The episode sits in the independent and critical quadrant, low on resource allocation and well above the midpoint on critical autonomy. The resource reading is modest: an independent, unsponsored podcast, with a guest who speaks from outside any current institutional or editorial seat. The autonomy reading is high because the conversation names what the field usually protects — the sold feature, the herd mechanism, the fair emptied of its own stated purpose — and does so while admitting the professional cost of saying it out loud. The position is that of a post-corporate insider spending the standing she built inside the industry to describe, plainly, what has stopped working in it.
